Valheim enemy AI: a mental model

Verified against decompiled Valheim 0.221.12 source.

The clock: MonoUpdaters

AI decisions run 20 times a second on one shared, global metronome. Execution (movement, physics, animation) runs every frame.

The shared MonoUpdaters instance stores the entire AI clock in one accumulator. Unity's FixedUpdate, the fixed-rate 50 Hz step that also runs physics, advances it: 

// MonoUpdaters.FixedUpdate: this is the entire AI clock
m_updateAITimer += fixedDeltaTime;         // 50 Hz fixed timestep
if (m_updateAITimer >= 0.05f) {            // ONE global accumulator → 20 Hz
    foreach (var ai in BaseAI.Instances)   // every loaded BaseAI, one flat list
        ai.UpdateAI(0.05f);
    m_updateAITimer -= 0.05f;
}

Every creature carries a BaseAI-derived component (MonsterAI for enemies, AnimalAI for passive animals), but no creature ticks itself: each registers into the static list BaseAI.Instances, and the loop above is the whole clock. Every brain in the world thinks in turn, on the same beat, 20 times a second, sequentially, on one thread.

The throttle only touches decisions: Character.Instances (movement, physics) ticks at the full 50 Hz and animation renders every frame, so fights look smooth however sparse the thinking is, and 50 ms sits far below human reaction time, so no player perceives the throttle itself. Between ticks a creature keeps executing its last choice, but at 50 ms that alone is nearly imperceptible; the commitment players feel in fights comes from slower layers, stacked in the Commitment section below. The throttle's real implication is a boundary: tactical behavior (which attack, how long to pursue, when to flee) belongs in UpdateAI, where 20 Hz is always enough, while anything frame-accurate (hit detection, parry timing, movement integration) lives in the execution layers and cannot be patched into the decision chain.

Design friction

The AI budget is shared. Every loaded brain runs in the same FixedUpdate call on one thread, so logic added inside UpdateAI is multiplied by every loaded creature, 20 times a second: a patch that is cheap on one Boar becomes a frame hitch when a raid event floods the base with thirty Greydwarfs.

Ownership: ZDO and ZNetView

A creature has exactly one brain running, on whichever machine owns it, and the brain can migrate between machines.

In multiplayer, no central machine simulates every creature. The world is peer-simulated: for each object, exactly one peer (the dedicated server or one player's client) runs its simulation, and every other peer just renders the result. Mapped onto the clock above: the "brain" is the creature's UpdateAI pass, and "owning" a creature means being the peer currently assigned to run that pass. That assignment lives in the creature's ZDO (zone data object).

The ZDO is the replicated key-value record behind every networked object; health, position, the alert flag, and star level all live there. ZNetView is the component that binds a live GameObject to its ZDO. Exactly one peer owns a given ZDO at a time: the owner simulates the object and writes its state, everyone else just reads. That is why the very first check in every AI pass is m_nview.IsOwner(): on non-owning machines BaseAI.UpdateAI returns immediately, running zero AI and mirroring the alert flag out of the ZDO. Ten players in a zone still means one brain per creature.

Ownership follows players around: the peer best placed to simulate a zone owns its objects, and when that changes (a player logs off, walks away, or a zone loads on the dedicated server) the brain migrates with it. The creature's state survives the move because it was never in the brain: it is in the ZDO, and the new owner picks up where the record left off. Leave a group fight and the friend still standing there inherits the Boar's brain mid-swing; the Boar never notices.

Design friction

Behavior is installed per machine, and the brain moves. When ownership migrates to a peer without the mod, the creature silently reverts to native behavior mid-fight; nothing errors. Changed behavior must ship to every peer that can own the creature (Benheim: the client mod for player-owned creatures, Benheim Test Commands for server-owned ones), and single-machine testing proves nothing about the group.

The pass: MonsterAI.UpdateAI

One tick evaluates the priority chain from top to bottom. It acts on the first match and returns. Control is stateless, but the data is stateful: every tick starts again at the top.

MonsterAI.UpdateAI(0.05f) is a plain function: a chain of guarded actions evaluated top to bottom, where the first condition that holds performs its one action and returns, and everything below it goes unevaluated that tick. A calm Boar grazing never reaches the attack rows; a cornered one falls straight through the flee checks to the bottom. A tick therefore executes a prefix of the chain, and which prefix depends entirely on what is true at that moment. There is no loop inside, no pointer into the chain, no saved position: control falls out of the function, and 50 ms later the clock re-enters it at the top.

What makes this workable is where state lives: the control flow is stateless, but the data is not. m_targetCreature stays set between ticks, the alert flag stays up in the ZDO, the circle-pause timer keeps accruing, and per-attack cooldowns keep counting on the attack items. A creature attacks the same player for ten straight seconds not because it remembers deciding to, but because the same persistent inputs keep producing the same decision, 20 times a second.

Expensive inputs refresh only at fixed intervals. UpdateTarget re-runs its enemy search only every 2 s (6 s with no player within 50 m), SelectBestAttack re-picks a weapon only every 1 s, and both wait out an attack in progress; between refreshes the chain reads the cached fields. The same pattern repeats at every scale: each attack item carries its own cooldown (m_aiAttackInterval), the creature has a global m_minAttackInterval, and circling and wander waypoints run on their own randomized timers. The chain itself runs every tick; stability comes from inputs that change more slowly by design. This is why a Greydwarf keeps hammering your friend for a beat after you step closer: it is not stubborn, its target search just has not fired yet.

Design friction

Reaction cadence is tunable, but not data-driven. Per-attack cooldowns are fields on the attack items. MonsterAI hardcodes the 2 s and 6 s target-search intervals and the 1 s weapon-selection interval for every creature. A mod can change those intervals only by patching MonsterAI. For a starred Boar, the behavior "detects players earlier and pursues longer" partly depends on reaction timing. The current rate limits protect the shared AI budget, so more frequent reactions spend more of that budget.

Interruption needs no stored-plan recovery. A stagger (Valheim's hit-reaction stun from heavy or parried damage) cannot corrupt a plan that was never stored. On the next tick, the priority chain derives an action from the current state. This is also the engine idiom behind Benheim's tier rule of deriving behavior from prefab and star level with no custom saved state: a modded creature stays correct through reload and ownership migration for the same reason a native one does.

Design friction

Order is load-bearing. Because a tick executes only a prefix, any condition added high in the chain shadows everything below it: a new flee rule can silently starve the attack rows. For modded behavior, where in the chain is as much a design decision as what.

Commitment: Attack and the Animator

The AI decision pass never waits and does not handle interruptions. The animation state machine owns commitment, and the next decision pass reads its state.

A swing takes half a second and a tick lasts 50 ms, so an attack outlives the tick that chose it and something other than the chain has to carry it. When the chain reaches DoAttack, Humanoid.StartAttack clones an Attack object from the chosen weapon item and starts its animation through the Animator (Unity's animation state machine, networked so every peer sees the same swing), then returns immediately: same tick, same thread. Nothing is dispatched to any asynchronous system. From that point the frame loop carries the swing, the Animator advancing the animation every frame with Attack.Update running alongside, and the damage lands when the animation reaches its hit event. A Troll's log swing keeps coming while you roll past it because the commitment is in the clip, not in any decision.

The Animator alone records whether an attack is active. InAttack() does not read an AI flag; it asks whether the current Animator state carries the attack tag. That is why the chain can keep ticking mid-swing without misbehaving: its guards read the Animator, see an attack in flight, and conclude there is nothing to decide yet. 

// the handoff: one tick, then done
DoAttack() → StartAttack():
    m_currentAttack = weapon.m_shared.m_attack.Clone();
    m_currentAttack.Start(...);   // kicks the animation, returns immediately

// every later tick, while the swing is in flight
InAttack()                        // asks the Animator for the attack tag
    → true: decision guards no-op; the chain "waits" by deciding nothing

// interruption, from the damage layer, any time
Character.Stagger() → m_zanim.SetTrigger("stagger");
    // Animator leaves the attack state → InAttack() == false
    // Attack.Update sees IsStaggering() → swing abandoned

Interruption works through the same door: a hard enough hit fires the animator's "stagger" trigger, the Animator leaves the attack-tagged state, and the swing is abandoned. MonsterAI contains no interruption handling at all: one layer shoves the Animator into a different state, and the chain observes different truth on its next tick. Stagger-based counter-play (Valheim's parry, Benheim's Perfect Impact) is this mechanism.

The commitment a player feels is therefore a stack of four mechanisms with very different strengths. The tick gap holds a choice for at most 50 ms and is nearly imperceptible. Sticky inputs (the pass section) reproduce the same choice for seconds at a time, but can flip on any tick. Movement inertia (Character.m_turnSpeed, m_acceleration) caps how fast the body can follow a changed decision: a charging Boar overshoots your sidestep because its turn rate is capped, not because it failed to notice you. And the animation lock owns a started attack outright until the clip ends or a stagger cuts it. Only the last two are commitments a player can exploit on purpose, and they live in different layers: dodging a charge beats the movement layer, dodging a swing beats the Animator.

Design friction

Attack pacing is not an AI knob. Speed, commitment, and interruptibility live in the animation layer: a faster swing means changing animation speed, and an uninterruptible one means a higher stagger threshold. The AI chooses only when a swing starts, never how it unfolds.
